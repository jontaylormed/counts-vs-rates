# Lesson 1: Which Place Has the Most Homelessness?

**One-line summary:** In one 45-minute period, students compare six places by raw count, then build the rate per 10,000 people themselves and see whether the ranking changes.

*Prints on about one page front and back. Data and sources: `sources.md` (Data set 4). Student file: `index.html`. Slides: `slides.html`.*

## Math skills and standards

**Math spine:** rate per 10,000 people (a unit rate), choosing a scale for a bar chart, rounding to a sensible accuracy, and arguing from data.

**Standards confirmed at thecorestandards.org (Common Core State Standards Initiative), pages fetched 2026-11-16:**

- **HSN-Q.A.1.** Use units to understand problems and guide solutions; choose and interpret units consistently; select and read scales and origins for graphs and data displays. (Rate units; chart scale.)
- **HSN-Q.A.2.** Define appropriate quantities for the purpose of descriptive modeling. (Why a rate, not a count, is the quantity for comparing places.)
- **HSN-Q.A.3.** Choose a level of accuracy appropriate to limitations on measurement when reporting quantities. (One decimal; the count is an undercount.)
- **MP3.** Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others.
- **MP4.** Model with mathematics.
- **Review, grades 6-7:** 6.RP.A.1 (ratio language), 6.RP.A.2 (unit rate), 6.RP.A.3 (ratio and rate reasoning), 7.RP.A.3 (proportional relationships for multistep ratio and percent problems).

## Learning targets ("I can ...")

1. I can explain why two places with different populations cannot be fairly compared by raw counts alone.
2. I can find a number in an original government report and say where it came from.
3. I can calculate a rate per 10,000 people and round it sensibly.
4. I can choose a scale and build a bar chart, and say how my scale choice changes what a viewer sees.
5. I can use the data, and what I know about how it was counted, to support a claim.

## Answer key

Source for every count: HUD 2024 Point-in-Time reports (HUD Exchange), January 2024 (`sources.md` table 4a). Source for every population: U.S. Census Bureau Vintage 2025 estimates, July 1, 2024. Rate = count / population x 10,000. The interactive accepts +/-0.5.

| Pierce County, WA | 2,661 | 939,786 | 2,661 / 939,786 x 10,000 = 28.31 | **28.3** |
| Multnomah County, OR | 7,384 | 794,472 | 7,384 / 794,472 x 10,000 = 92.94 | **92.9** |
| San Francisco, CA | 8,323 | 820,945 | 8,323 / 820,945 x 10,000 = 101.38 | **101.4** |
| San Diego County, CA | 10,605 | 3,287,542 | 10,605 / 3,287,542 x 10,000 = 32.26 | **32.3** |
| Maricopa County, AZ | 9,435 | 4,654,147 | 9,435 / 4,654,147 x 10,000 = 20.27 | **20.3** |
| *United States (worked example)* | 771,480 | 340,003,797 | 771,480 / 340,003,797 x 10,000 = 22.69 | **22.7** (HUD prints "about 23 of every 10,000," AHAR 2024 Part 1) |

**Ranking by raw count (most to fewest):** 1 King (16,868), 2 San Diego (10,605), 3 Maricopa (9,435), 4 San Francisco (8,323), 5 Multnomah (7,384), 6 Pierce (2,661).

**Ranking by rate (highest to lowest):** 1 San Francisco (101.4), 2 Multnomah (92.9), 3 King (72.8), 4 San Diego (32.3), 5 Pierce (28.3), 6 Maricopa (20.3).

Biggest moves: San Francisco 4th to 1st; Maricopa 3rd to 6th; Multnomah 5th to 2nd. A student who rounds to a whole number gets the same order. Calculation mistakes to expect: dividing population by count (gives about 0.01), or forgetting x 10,000.

## Discussion notes (grounded only in caveats printed in sources.md)

**1. Did the ranking change?** Yes (see moves above). Let students name which places moved and by how much. Do not tell them what it means.

**2. Why do counts and rates tell different stories?** A count says how many; a rate says how many for each 10,000 people living there, so a large place and a small place can be compared. Maricopa and San Diego have large populations, which lowers their rates. Ask students which question each number answers, and when each is the better one. Bring in the scale choice from Step 3: the same data can look different with a different axis maximum.

**3. Who might this count miss?** All from sources.md "How this was counted":

- **One night only.** PIT is a snapshot in late January, not everyone who experiences homelessness in a year. KCRHA says it "is broadly understood to be an undercount."
- **Narrow definition.** PIT counts people in shelter or transitional housing, or in places not meant for sleeping (streets, vehicles, parks). People staying doubled-up with friends or family are not counted.
- **Methods differ by place.** HUD's AHAR says "there is no universal method"; King County uses a sample survey (RDS), which KCRHA says is "likely an undercount." Children under 18 who are on their own are estimated, not interviewed.
- **Dates and areas.** PIT dates are in late January 2024; Census population is July 1, 2024. The match between each HUD area and each county is the researcher's reading of the names, not a HUD statement. The U.S. total includes territories; the Census U.S. population covers the 50 states and DC only. That may be part of why the U.S. rate is 22.7 and HUD prints "about 23."
- **Sheltering differs by place.** AHAR notes unsheltered rates tend to be higher in warmer climates, and shelter practices differ. (New York is the extension below.)

## Care for students

Some students have lived this, or are living it now. Use person-first language ("people experiencing homelessness"). Never ask anyone to disclose. Reflection sharing is optional, and students may write about the data only. If a student discloses something, listen, thank them, and follow your school's procedure; connect them with a counselor. Do not promise to keep it secret. Do not read private reflections aloud.

## If time runs short

Cut Step 5 (Reflect) to homework: it can be done and exported at home. Do not cut Step 3; it is the math. If Step 2 is blocked by web filters, use the fallback and show the HUD page on the projector.

## Optional extension

New York City and Los Angeles need extra steps (sources.md table 4a). **New York City:** add the populations of five counties (Bronx 1,406,052; Kings 2,658,657; New York 1,665,510; Queens 2,367,034; Richmond 499,572) and compare with HUD's count of 140,134. Note that 132,892 people were in emergency shelter. **Los Angeles County:** add four HUD CoCs (Los Angeles 71,201; Long Beach 3,376; Pasadena 556; Glendale 175) to match the county population of 9,748,868. The researcher inferred, and HUD did not state, that the four cover the county. The lesson: the counted area must match the population area.
