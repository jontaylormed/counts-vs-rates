Lesson 1

Which place has the most homelessness?

How would you decide? What numbers would you need?

Today: real numbers from HUD and the U.S. Census Bureau.

Read the question aloud. Take two or three quick answers; do not say which is right. Tell students they will commit to an answer from raw counts first, then test it. Remind them we say "people experiencing homelessness."

What is a Point-in-Time (PIT) count?

  • A count of people experiencing homelessness on one night in late January.
  • It counts people in shelter or transitional housing and people staying outside or in places not meant for sleeping (streets, vehicles, parks).
  • It does not count people staying with friends or family (“doubled-up”).
  • Each local area chooses its method, so methods differ from place to place.

Sources: HUD, 2024 Annual Homelessness Assessment Report (AHAR) Part 1; KCRHA 2024 PIT Count FAQ.

Keep it short. A PIT count is a one-night snapshot, not everyone who experiences homelessness over a year. KCRHA's FAQ says the PIT count "is broadly understood to be an undercount." HUD's AHAR says there is no universal method: each local area chooses among approved methods. The definition leaves out doubled-up people. Ask: who might a one-night count miss? Park that question; students return to it in Step 4.

Today's steps (about 45 minutes)

  1. Meet the data (5 min)
  2. Verify in the original report (7)
  3. Organize: build a table and two bar charts (15)
  4. Analyze (8)
  5. Reflect and hand in (7)

I can…

  • decide what makes a comparison between places fair
  • find a number in the original source
  • turn raw numbers into a comparison I can defend
  • choose a scale and build a bar chart
  • use data to support a claim
Everyone works on their own laptop in the interactive. Step 2: students find King County's number and one other in the HUD report; there is a "Show me where" hint and an "I can't open this site" fallback. Step 3 is the math: they compute each rate themselves, then choose each chart's scale. Reflection sharing is optional. Do not mention rates yet: students commit to an answer from raw counts first. Tell students where the file is and start Step 1. STOP the slides here. Come back for slide 4 when most students reach Step 3.

The idea: a rate per 10,000 people

Worked example: the United States, 2024

771,480 people experiencing homelessness ÷ 340,003,797 people living in the U.S.

771,480 ÷ 340,003,797 × 10,000 ≈ 22.7

About 23 people for every 10,000 people. HUD prints “about 23 of every 10,000.”

Your turn: the same steps for six places. Do you expect the order to stay the same?

Sources: HUD 2024 PIT report, all states, territories, Puerto Rico and DC (771,480); U.S. Census Bureau Vintage 2025, July 1, 2024 (340,003,797); HUD AHAR 2024 Part 1 (“about 23 of every 10,000”).

Show this slide when most students reach Step 3, not during the launch, so their first answer comes from raw counts. Walk through the division slowly: count divided by population gives a tiny decimal (about 0.00227); multiplying by 10,000 turns it into "people per 10,000," which is easier to read. Do not do the six places for them. Note for students who ask why 22.7 and HUD says about 23: the HUD count includes U.S. territories and Puerto Rico, while the Census population is the 50 states and DC only (sources.md, Data set 4), and 22.7 rounds to about 23. Do not predict the order for the class.

Closing discussion

  • Did the ranking change when you used rates instead of counts? What moved?
  • When is a count the better number? When is a rate?
  • Who might a one-night count miss?
  • What question would you want data to answer next?

Turn to a neighbor for one minute, then we'll hear from a few volunteers. Passing is always fine.

Let students draw the conclusions. Reflect back what they say with the data ("so the rate for X was..."). Do not tell them what the numbers mean. Caveats students may raise are all in sources.md: one night only; doubled-up people not counted; methods differ by place; count dates are January while Census population is July 1. Sharing is optional. If a student shares something personal, thank them, keep the discussion on the data, and follow your school's procedure afterward.
