# Sources and verified data: Homelessness in King County, WA (Lesson 01)

**Date checked for every figure in this file: 2026-11-16.**

Every number in the data tables below was read from a page or file that was opened during this research session. Nothing comes from memory. Anything that could not be opened or confirmed is listed under "Unverified / could not confirm" at the end.

**Labels used in tables**

- **Raw** means a count or dollar amount exactly as the source prints it.
- **Published %** means a percentage or rate that the source itself prints. The researcher did not calculate it.
- **Sum (researcher)** means the researcher added two raw numbers from the same source, such as emergency shelter plus transitional housing. These sums are labeled wherever they appear.
- No rates per 10,000, percent changes or shares were calculated for this file. Students calculate those.

**About the search phrases.** Each source has a short phrase a student can type into a search engine. "Tested" means the phrase was run in a web search during this session and the source (or a page that links straight to it) came up in the results. "Not tested" means the phrase was not run. Some tables on data.census.gov do not show up in general search engines. For those, the steps for the site's own search box are given instead.

### How this was counted and who may be missing (Data set 1)

- **One night only.** The PIT count is a one-night snapshot, not a count of everyone who is homeless during a year. KCRHA's 2024 FAQ (S6) says the PIT count "is broadly understood to be an undercount." It also says Washington's Department of Commerce estimated "nearly 54,000 people experienced homelessness in King County over the course of a year." The 2022 infographic (S7) cites a King County DCHS analysis finding "at least 40,871 people experienced homelessness at some point in 2020."
- **The old street count missed people.** KCRHA (S6) describes the traditional count as volunteers counting in areas "unfamiliar to them, recorded on a paper tally sheet," often in heavy rain or cold, and says the hand count "results in an undercount."
- **RDS is an estimate.** RDS "is not a census, but a rigorous data sample" (S3). The 2024 report (S4, p. 12) says RDS "is still likely an undercount."
- **Children are estimated, not interviewed.** The RDS survey was approved only for adults 18 and over. Unsheltered unaccompanied youth under 18 are estimated with statistical methods from shelter data (S4, p. 12).
- **The definition is narrow.** PIT counts only people in shelters, transitional housing or safe havens (sheltered), or in places "not meant for human habitation" such as streets, vehicles and parks (unsheltered). People staying "doubled-up" with friends or family are **not** counted. Compare this with the school data in Data set 6, where doubled-up students are the largest group.
- **HUD's caution.** Every HUD report (S11) says: "a shift in the methodology a CoC uses to count the homeless may cause a change in homeless counts between reporting periods," and HUD "has not independently verified all of the information."
- **Pandemic years.** AHAR (S12) says comparisons with pandemic years (2020-2022) "should be made with caution."
- **Big method breaks to show students:** 2016 to 2017 (street method changed), 2020 to 2022 (RDS replaced the street count, and 2021 had no unsheltered count), and the repeated unsheltered numbers in 2023 and 2025.

## (b) Discrepancies between sources

1. **Families in 2024: KCRHA vs. HUD.**
   - HUD 2024 PopSub shows 3,701 persons in households with at least one adult and one child (526 unsheltered) and 1,037 such households.
   - KCRHA's revised figures (S2, S4, March 2026) show 4,863 individuals in families and 1,428 family households. The 2024 report says unsheltered people in adult-and-child households rose "from 1,253 to 1,688."
   - KCRHA revised the 2024 family estimates in March 2026. The HUD report is dated December 2024. The overall total (16,868) is the same in both.
2. **"415 households" in 2022.** The 2026 Executive Report (S2) says unsheltered families went "from 1,253 individuals in 415 households" in 2022. But the 2024 report (S4, p. 42) says 415 was the 2022 estimate of individuals in **Children Only** households, not family households. One of these is likely a labeling error. Ask students to notice it.
3. **Shelter bed baseline year.** S2 says beds fell from 5,958 to 5,269 "between 2025 and 2026." The 2026 FAQ (S3) says "between 2024 and 2026." HUD shows 5,173 in 2024 and 5,958 in 2025, so "2025 to 2026" matches HUD.
4. **2023 HIC total.** HUD prints 19,809. KCRHA's chart (S2, p. 7) prints 19,733. The 2022, 2024 and 2025 totals match.
5. **2018 total in the 2020 report.** Count Us In 2020 (S8) prints 12,112 in Figure 1 but "N=12,122" in Figure 2. HUD shows 12,112.
6. **2026 survey sites and volunteers.** The FAQ (S3) says 25 hub sites and more than 200 volunteers. The Executive Report footnote (S2) says "nearly 30 different locations with over 150 community volunteers."
7. **2024 hub count.** KCRHA's PIT page says 19 hub sites. The 2024 Summary FAQ says "20 total hubs."
8. **The census year KCRHA cites for 2026.** Page 4 of S2 says "American Community Survey Census Reference (2025)." Footnote 4 cites "American Community Survey 5-Year Estimates" 2024, Table DP05.
9. **KCRHA census shares vs. Census table B03002.** KCRHA prints King County as 54.4% white, 19.7% Asian and 6.9% multiracial. Researcher's calculation from B03002 2020-2024 5-year (shown only to explain the mismatch, not for the lesson): 1,210,898 / 2,287,171 is about 52.9% non-Hispanic white; Asian is about 20.5%; two or more races is about 7.3%. The Black (6.4%) and Hispanic (about 11.0% vs. 10.8%) shares are close. KCRHA probably used a different table or grouping (DP05). Students using B03002 will get slightly different shares than KCRHA's.
10. **Census vs. OFM population.** For April 1, 2024, OFM gives 2,378,100. For July 1, 2024, Census gives 2,317,959. Per-capita rates will differ depending on which series students use.
11. **Census 2010 and 2020 values differ by vintage.** For 2010: 1,937,961 (intercensal) vs. 1,938,431 (Vintage 2020). For 2020: 2,274,315 (Vintage 2020) vs. 2,274,304 (Vintage 2025).
12. **2013 and 2014 unsheltered are identical in HUD (2,736).** HUD requires a sheltered count every year but an unsheltered count only every other year (S4, p. 11; S2, fn. 1). The repeated value suggests 2014 reused 2013's unsheltered number, as happened in 2023 and 2025. This is not confirmed (see Unverified item 4).
13. **Students: OSPI report vs. Report Card.** For 2024-25 statewide, the legislative report (S24) shows 48,252 and the Report Card file (S26) shows 43,542. They use different counting rules (see Data set 6).
14. **Count dates.** HUD dates the 2017 count 1/26/2017 and the 2020 count 1/23/2020. The local reports describe the street count on the *morning* of January 27, 2017 and January 24, 2020. These are the same night described two ways.

## (c) Which data sets are richest for student analysis

1. **The PIT time series 2007-2025/2026 (Data set 1) with population (Data set 3)** is the best set for per-capita rates, percent change and modeling change over time. It also has clear method breaks (2017, 2021-2022, and the repeated unsheltered numbers in 2014, 2023 and 2025). Students can see how a change in counting can look like a change in homelessness, which supports critiquing a graph.
2. **The 2024 HUD race counts (2b) next to Census B03002 (2c)** give raw numbers on both sides, so students compute both shares themselves and compare them. The HUD and Census categories line up well. The 2026 KCRHA percentages (2a), plus KCRHA's own warning about the jump in the White share, are a strong lesson on how category and method changes affect conclusions.
3. **The 2024 comparison table (Data set 4)** works well for rates per 10,000 people. Students can check their U.S. rate against HUD's published "about 23 per 10,000." The LA and NYC rows teach that you must match the counted area to the right population.
4. **Rent and income 2010-2025 (Data set 5)** suits percent-change comparisons (rent growth vs. income growth). It also supports a rent-to-income ratio using a monthly rent × 12 conversion (unit rates, HSN-Q). FMR adds a second measure that sometimes moves the opposite way.
5. **The school data (Data set 6)** connects directly to students' lives. It supports district rates per 100 or 1,000 students, and the doubled-up vs. HUD-definition contrast is a strong "who gets counted" discussion. Handle it with care. Use district totals only, never school-level data. Federal Way's year-to-year swing is a good "check the data before concluding" moment.
